# Craft: render the world, embody the people (don't just name them)

**Principle.** Worldbuilding on the page is not naming systems ("the Weave", "the
Blight", "Classes") — it's making the world PERCEIVABLE and LIVED: what it looks/
sounds/smells like, how ordinary people live under its rules, the concrete specifics
(a food, a street sound, a ritual, an object). Likewise a character is built not by a
trait list but by a BODY in motion: a face, a build, a signature mannerism that
recurs and carries meaning. Reveal both through action and the POV's perception, never
as an info-dump. (Sources: Le Guin, *Steering the Craft*; Card, *How to Write SF&F* —
the MICE/“dig the well” of worldbuilding; Bell on character tags/traits.)

**Why the model needs it.** Left alone it NAMES (efficient, abstract) instead of
RENDERING (concrete, sensory), and it sketches characters as functions. The bible's
`WORLDBUILDING` section + the required per-character `physicality` force the material
to exist; the cold read's immersion/embodiment lens checks it reached the page. See
references/failures/sparse-underbuilt-prose.md.

**Technique — worldbuilding through use, not exposition.**
- Tie the rule to a daily, sensory consequence: not "Classes grant power" but a sigil
  stitched on the right sleeve that people read before your face; the sleeveless take
  the work nobody's Class wants.
- Give the place a sensory signature (river-stone gone grey with damp; the dawn
  Class-bell counting the city by rank) and let the POV move through it.
- One concrete object can carry a world: a flat iron token, blank where a sigil should
  be, still warm from another discarded palm.

**Technique — embodiment.**
- Each major character: a rendered body + ONE recurring mannerism that does emotional
  work (Veil turns her reading-glass when calm; her thumb presses white on it when she's
  hiding something). The mannerism, reused, becomes characterisation.
- Embody the POV too — easy to forget, since we're inside them. In a scene about being
  judged, show how the POV LOOKS to the room.
- Let secondary characters speak at least once; a described type isn't a person yet.

**Discriminating check.** For each named character in the scene: is there a body, a
face, or a mannerism — or just a name and lines? For the setting: can the reader
perceive it through ≥2 senses, and is at least one of the world's rules shown as lived
consequence? `texture` gives the density floor; the eye/cold-read judges whether it's
actually rendered.
