# Craft: show, don't tell (and when to tell)

**Principle.** Dramatise what matters; summarise what doesn't. *Telling* names a
conclusion ("she was furious"); *showing* gives the reader the evidence and lets
them conclude ("she set the cup down without a sound and did not look at him
again"). Telling creates distance and denies the reader the pleasure of inference.
But telling is the right tool for transitions and low-value information — *show*
the emotional beats, *tell* the drive across town. (Sources: Gardner, *The Art of
Fiction*; Burroway, *Writing Fiction*.)

**Why the model needs it.** The most-probable continuation of an emotional moment
is to *name the emotion* and reach for a body-cliché ("her heart pounded"). That's
telling twice over. `prose_scan` flags told-emotion words and the body clichés;
filter words (felt/saw/realised) are the seam where telling leaks in.

**Before (told + filtered + cliché).**
> She felt terrified. Her heart pounded. She realised the door was open and knew
> someone had been inside.

**After (shown).**
> The door stood open a hand's width. She had locked it. On the table her papers
> lay fanned out like a dealt hand — and the top drawer, the one that stuck, was
> closed.

No named emotion, no filter verb, no pounding heart; the dread is in the specifics.

**Discriminating check.** Circle every named emotion and every filter verb. For each,
ask: can the reader infer this from an action, object, or line of dialogue instead?
If yes, and the beat matters, show it. If the beat is just transition, a plain tell
is fine — don't fetishise showing the trip to the shops.
